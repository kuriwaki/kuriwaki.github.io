## What we tried

From sitting in the class, I observe substantial engagement from students during lectures, as they asked questions, contributed their experiences, and responded to one another’s ideas. Shiro also uses pre-class quizzes to involve students in experiments, and those results become examples during lecture to illustrate a point. While class engagement is not an issue on the whole, we still wanted to encourage students who are more hesitant to speak in class.

One of the initiatives introduced this semester is cold calling, so Shiro can sample understanding across the classroom, including students who may not volunteer. He begins at a randomly chosen point in the room and then moves through students in seating order. Most exchanges are brief, but he sometimes stays with a student, offering follow-up questions and prompts to help them develop an answer. The aim is both to understand students’ thinking and to scaffold their reasoning toward a clearer explanation.

## What we noticed

Since this is the first time our class is trying out the cold-calling, we learned a few insights. It is very important to ask clear questions, but before even starting with the questions, sharing an example of how to approach the problem is helpful. When students are not sure how to respond, they will say they don’t know or their body language will show their confusion. Being quick to move onto the next student is also important so it does not bog down the momentum of the class. Other times, Shiro and the student engage in a back-and-forth; a recap for the rest of the class to follow the reasoning is helpful.

When I interviewed one of the students about their experience in the class, especially how they feel about the cold-calling, they mentioned they appreciated the supportive approach to cold calling since they feel like it’s okay if they don’t know. Still, they suggested that concepts should be fully explained before students were asked to apply them.

## What we would change

Prepare a short sequence of cold-call questions in advance during lecture preparation. Since coming up with the questions on the spot could create more confusion if it is not a clear question or in logical sequence.

For a key concept, give students a moment to think individually, then open the class to the turn-and-talk with a neighbor before calling on students to share. This gives everyone an opportunity to attempt the reasoning, compare their approaches, and rehearse an explanation.

Trying to scaffold a student’s response could be helpful, as the answer gets warmer or closer to the mark, other students could be inspired to answer correctly.
